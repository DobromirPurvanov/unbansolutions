Short answer: the "verification link" scam is phishing. You get a message supposedly from Instagram, Meta or TikTok saying a blue badge, a partnership or a violation appeal is waiting for you, and the link leads to a fake login page that steals your password and your confirmation code. Platforms never send such offers in direct messages. If you have already entered your details, you have minutes rather than days: change the password, end the intruder's sessions, and go through the official flow for hacked accounts.

## How the scheme works, step by step

The scam is an assembly line and looks almost identical in all its variants:

1. **The hook.** A direct message, an email or a comment: "Your account has been approved for a blue badge", "A copyright violation was found, appeal here within 24 hours", or "Brand X wants a paid partnership with you". The tone always pairs a reward or a threat with urgency.
2. **The set.** The link leads to a page copied pixel by pixel from the real one: the logo, the fonts, even the privacy text. The difference is in the address: instead of instagram.com you see something like instagram-verify-badge.com, meta-support-help.net, or a shortened link that hides the final address.
3. **The harvest.** You enter your email and password. The page also asks for the six-digit code "for confirmation" — that is, your two-factor code, which arrives at that very moment from the real platform, because the scammer is already logging in with your password.
4. **The lockout.** Within minutes the email and phone number on the account are changed, your sessions are ended, and the profile is often renamed. Then come messages to your contacts with the same bait, or a ransom demand to you.

The whole cycle from the click to losing access takes under ten minutes. That is why the speed of your reaction decides the outcome.

## The signs that give a fake message away

- **The platform messages you first in DMs.** Instagram, Meta and TikTok communicate through in-app notifications and email from their own domains, never through direct messages from profiles like "Meta Support Team 2026".
- **The address is not the exact domain.** The real ones are instagram.com, facebook.com, tiktok.com and their subdomains. Anything with a hyphen, an added word or a different ending belongs to someone else. On a phone, press and hold the link to see the full address before opening it.
- **Urgency with a deadline.** "Within 24 hours", "immediately", "final warning". Real violation notices sit in the app and do not expire in hours.
- **A confirmation code is requested.** A 2FA code is entered only in the app itself or on the official site at login. Any other place asking for it is stealing it.
- **Small language errors.** Increasingly rare now that scammers write with language models, so do not rely on this sign alone.

## The other masks of the same scam

The verification link is only the most popular costume. The same mechanism also appears as:

- **"Copyright violation".** A message that your page will be deleted within 24 hours over a complaint, with a link to "appeal". It plays on fear and works particularly well against business pages. A real copyright claim looks different and appears in a specific place in the app — see [removal on a claim](/vidove-sanktsii#ip-copyright).
- **An advertising or partnership invitation.** "Brand X invites you to a paid collaboration, see the brief here." The file is malware, or the link asks you to log in with your account.
- **A Business Manager invitation.** A genuine Meta feature used maliciously: accept an invitation from an unknown "partner" and you give them a role over your assets. Check every invitation in Business Settings before accepting it.
- **"Release your reserved username."** A message that someone has requested your name and you must "confirm ownership". No platform has such a procedure.
- **Fake support in the comments.** Under a post where you complain about a problem, a "Help Center Team" profile appears, ready to "assist" in DMs. Real support does not work in comments.

The common denominator of every variant: they want you to leave the platform through their link and type in your details. Everything legitimate happens inside the app, without exception.

## If you manage client accounts

For agencies and freelancers the risk is multiplied: one successful phishing attempt against you opens all your clients' accounts. The minimum we recommend: a separate work profile for access with no public visibility, roles through Business Manager instead of shared passwords (never, under any circumstances, passwords shared over chat), 2FA on every account, and a monthly review of who has rights to what. A client contract that settles who is responsible in a breach is not a luxury either.

## I clicked the link. What now?

It depends how far you got.

**You only opened the page without entering anything.** The risk is small. Close it, download nothing, and change your password through the app as a precaution.

**You entered a password but not a code.** Change the password immediately, from the app or the official site. Then, in the security settings, review the active sessions and end every unfamiliar one. Turn on two-factor authentication if you do not have it.

**You entered a password and a code, or you can no longer log in.** Act in this order:

1. Try logging in and "Forgot password" right away. If your email is still attached, you will get ahead of the change.
2. Search your inbox for a message from the platform about a changed email. Instagram and Facebook emails contain a "secure your account" / "revert this change" link that restores the old value. That link is the shortest path and works for days after the change.
3. Go through the official flow for hacked accounts: [instagram.com/hacked](https://www.instagram.com/hacked/) or [facebook.com/hacked](https://www.facebook.com/hacked). You enter with a username or an old email and reach identity verification, including a video selfie for accounts with photos of your face.
4. On TikTok, use the feedback form with the description "hacked account" and the profile details.
5. Tell your followers through another channel that scammers are writing from your profile, to break the chain.

Do not pay a ransom. In the cases we have seen, paying almost never restores access — it marks you as a paying victim for a second attempt.

## How to make the next attempt fail

The human factor is the front door of most breaches; recent editions of the annual Verizon DBIR report place it in around two thirds of incidents. The technical side is simple:

- **Two-factor authentication through an app** (Google Authenticator, Authy) rather than SMS. SMS codes are vulnerable to SIM swapping and get typed into fake pages out of habit.
- **A passkey wherever the platform supports it.** A passkey cannot be entered on a fake site, which kills phishing by design.
- **A unique password for every platform**, stored in a password manager. A leaked password from an old forum should not open your Instagram.
- **A review of connected apps** every few months: delete everything you do not use.
- **The one-click-away rule:** whatever notification you receive, do not enter through its link — open the app and check there. Real notices are always visible inside as well.

## Frequently asked questions

### The hacker changed both the email and the phone number. Is the account lost?

Not necessarily. Platforms keep a history of changes and have processes for proving identity: the emails that restore an old address, video selfie verification, an ID document. The chance drops with time, because thieves clean up the traces, so start the procedure on day one.

### I had two-factor authentication on. How was I hacked at all?

The phishing page asked for the six-digit code too, and you entered it there; the code works once, but once is enough. The other common scenario is the theft of an active session through malware on your device. 2FA protects against known passwords, not against handing over the key yourself.

### The verification message came from a profile with a blue badge. Doesn't that make it real?

No. The badge confirms the identity of the person behind that profile, not that they speak for the platform. Scammers buy or steal verified accounts precisely to sound credible.

### Should I report this anywhere outside the platform?

In Bulgaria you can file a report with the cybercrime unit of GDBOP (cybercrime.bg), especially where there is financial damage or extortion. Document everything with screenshots before the profile and the messages are deleted.
