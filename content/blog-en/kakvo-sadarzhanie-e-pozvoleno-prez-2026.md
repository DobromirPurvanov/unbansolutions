Short answer: in 2026 platforms work with three levels. Prohibited content, which gets removed and penalizes the account: violence, harassment, scams, restricted goods, sexual content, dangerous misinformation. Borderline content, which stays online but is not recommended: clickbait, shocking and sexualized shots, dubious health advice. And a new obligation many people miss: realistic AI content must be labelled, and unlabelled AI is starting to be treated as misleading.

The difference between the three levels explains most reach "mysteries" and most surprising bans. Here is the map. Topic by topic, with before-and-after examples, the same ground is covered in the [organic content rules](/pravila/organichno-sadarzhanie).

## The "removed" level: the hard bans

The main categories have been stable for years and are shared by Meta, TikTok and YouTube, with differences in the detail:

- **Violence and dangerous organizations.** Threats, incitement, praise for terrorist and criminal structures.
- **Harassment and hate speech.** Attacks on protected groups, systematic harassment of a specific person, exposure of personal data.
- **Sexual content and nudity.** With narrow exceptions (art, health topics, breastfeeding) that algorithms do not always recognize on the first pass; which is why many appeals here succeed.
- **Scams and impersonation.** Fake promotions, get-rich-quick schemes, posing as another person or brand.
- **Restricted goods and services.** Drugs, weapons, prescription medicines, and in advertising: strict regimes for alcohol, gambling, financial services and health products.
- **Dangerous misinformation.** Medical claims that endanger health, and manipulation around elections.
- **Copyright.** Third-party video, music and images without rights. Repeat violations take accounts down, including large ones.

Penalties escalate in steps: a removed post, a warning, limited features, a suspended account. The history is kept, which is why two removed posts within a month weigh far more than two across three years.

## The "not recommended" level: the grey zone that cuts reach

This is the most common misunderstanding. The post stays online, nobody has formally penalized you, but the platform has stopped showing it to anyone outside your followers. Meta calls this "borderline content"; TikTok maintains a list of what is "ineligible for the For You feed". The grey zone covers:

- clickbait headlines and engagement bait ("comment YES if…");
- sexualized but not nude shots;
- shocking and risky scenes, dangerous challenges;
- health and diet claims without context, weight-loss content aimed at minors;
- exaggerated financial promises;
- recycled content carrying another platform's watermark.

If an account produces such content systematically, the limit spreads across the whole profile. What that looks like from inside and how to check it is covered in the article on [shadowbans](/blog/shadowban-kakvo-e-kak-da-go-razpoznaete-i-premahnete).

## New in 2026: AI content needs a label

The rules here tightened noticeably. Realistic images, video and audio created or substantially altered with AI have to be labelled: Meta attaches an "AI info" label and requires a declaration when uploading realistic synthetic video, TikTok mandates the label for realistic AI content and builds in automatic detection from metadata. In the EU, the transparency obligations under the AI Act turned labelling from a platform rule into a regulatory requirement.

The practical translation: AI content is allowed, business use included, as long as it does not present itself as real. What gets removed and penalized are deepfakes of real people without consent, a synthetic voice of a public figure, "documentary" footage of events that never happened. An AI-rendered product in an ad that looks different from the real one falls under misleading content, with the corresponding consequences for the ad account.

If you use AI tools for content, tick the declaration at upload whenever the result is realistic. The label does not reduce reach by default; hidden synthetic content, detected later, reduces trust in the entire account.

## The traps for business accounts

The most expensive mistakes we see are not from influencers but from small businesses, because there a violation hits the ad account too. The persistent problem zones:

1. **Result promises in the aesthetic and health sectors.** Before-and-after shots, guaranteed results, and targeting by personal attributes ("for you who have…") are restricted in Meta ads. The post passes organically, but the same text in an ad cuts the account.
2. **Finance and crypto.** Many formats require prior permission or a licence; without them, rejected ads pile up against the profile.
3. **Music in business content.** Commercial accounts do not hold the rights that personal ones have to popular music in the libraries. A promo video scored with someone else's music is a copyright violation.
4. **Contests and giveaways.** Requiring shares to stories and tagging friends breaks the spam rules in some formats; the wording matters.
5. **Bought-in content.** Posting the same viral clips that hundreds of pages are running classifies the account as unoriginal and pulls it out of recommendations and monetization.

## Quick differences between platforms

If you publish the same content everywhere, keep in mind which rule cuts hardest where.

**Instagram** is the most sensitive to sexualized content and engagement bait; the grey zone cuts reach faster there than elsewhere. Plus a link specific: accounts that systematically push external links to dubious domains lose visibility wholesale.

**TikTok** leads on strictness toward dangerous challenges and content involving minors; there, age restrictions on videos (hidden from under-18s) also arrive automatically and quietly. Music is freer for personal accounts, but business accounts may only use the commercial library.

**Facebook** is strictest on health and political misinformation and on recycled page content; groups carry separate responsibility for admins who let violating posts through.

**YouTube** works with a strike system: three in 90 days take the channel down, but the first only freezes uploading. Copyright there has the sharpest automation (Content ID), so music is the first thing you check before uploading.

## Monetization: the higher bar

Everything above applies to ordinary posting. Monetized accounts pass through a second, stricter filter: content that is allowed but "not advertiser-friendly" loses revenue without any violation at all. Coarse language in the first seconds, contentious topics, even excessive footage of accidents lower the ad rate. If platform revenue matters to you, read each platform's separate monetization rules, not just the community standards; the two diverge exactly where it hurts.

## How to publish calmly: our working list

Before a post that feels like it is on the edge, run four questions. Does it show something you would not put on a billboard? Does it promise a result you cannot prove? Does it use someone else's material without rights? Is the AI involvement realistic, and is it labelled? Four "no" answers mean a calm publication.

And the habit that feeds everything else: check Account Status once a month and appeal wrong decisions immediately, instead of letting them accumulate in the history. The rules will keep moving; your account's history stays.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is fully AI-generated content allowed?

Yes, on all the major platforms, under two conditions: realistic output is labelled and does not mislead (no fake events, no third-party faces or voices without consent). Stylized and obviously unrealistic AI content needs no label.

### Is swearing and coarse language banned?

Not in itself. It becomes a problem when aimed at a specific person or group (harassment, hate speech) or in content reaching minors; that content drops out of both recommendations and monetization.

### Can I repost someone else's videos with credit?

Crediting the author does not replace rights. Without permission or licensed material you risk a copyright report, and systematic reposting classifies the account as unoriginal. The only safe route is your own content or explicit consent.

### Are the rules different for small accounts?

The rules are the same, but enforcement differs: large accounts go through human review more often, while small ones are entirely in the hands of automation. That is why small accounts more often take wrong penalties — and more often win on appeal.
